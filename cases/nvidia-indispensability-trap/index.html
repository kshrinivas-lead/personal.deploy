The Indispensability Trap: Nvidia’s 26 Days

A case study and an argument

By Shrinivas Kulkarni·As of 28 November 2026Developing — investigations open

A great CEO builds a great company. A greater CEO builds one that doesn’t need them.

How to read this. Part 1 is the case: what happened, the evidence, and the decisions the board faced. It is written neutrally, with pauses to ask what you would have done. Part 2 is my argument. Part 3 turns both into lessons and questions for your own board or leadership team. Read straight through as an article, or stop at each pause if you’re using it for discussion.

All allegations are as reported by the outlets named. None of the espionage claims has been established. China, Singapore and the company involved deny them, and investigations are still open.

Part 1

The case

The decision on the table

On the weekend of 22 November 2026, the directors of Nvidia, then a roughly $4 trillion company and the central supplier of the AI boom, had to decide whether to reverse themselves.

Eleven days earlier they had accepted the resignation of Jensen Huang, who co-founded the company in 1993 and had been its only CEO. Five days earlier their new chair had told investors, on the record, that the board did not intend to reinstate him. Now the founder was publicly offering to return, a petition with more than 180,000 signatures was demanding it, the stock had lost hundreds of billions of dollars, and the President of the United States was urging the board to give him a second chance. The independent investigation the board had commissioned had not reported.

How did they get here?

Background: the company and the board

Nvidia began as a graphics-chip company and became the backbone of AI computing. Under Huang it reached about $216 billion in revenue in fiscal 2026. Huang was president, CEO and a director, and he was widely seen as the face of the AI revolution.

The board was built around him. Until the crisis there was no board chair. The most senior director, Harvey C. Jones, had served since 1993, before the company went public. Several directors had known Huang for decades. There was no chief compliance officer. Colette Kress, CFO since 2013, ran finance and the earnings calls, and Huang handled the vision and the big relationships.

The crisis unfolds

On 29 October the New York Times reported a close relationship between Huang and a former executive of Megaspeed, a Singapore company that had reportedly bought about $4.8 billion of Nvidia chips. Within days, the Wall Street Journal reported that intelligence officials in several countries suspected her of working for Chinese intelligence, and congressional committees asked Huang to testify.

⏸ Decision point 1 · 8 November

Huang tells employees he was unfaithful to his wife. The reports allege he was the target of a foreign intelligence effort. Nothing has been proven, and he denies knowing of any spying.

Your options as a director

  • asay nothing and wait
  • bopen an independent investigation and keep him as CEO
  • copen an investigation and put him on leave
  • dask for his resignation

What would you do, and what would each option signal to employees, investors and Washington?

Decide first, then see what the board didWhat the board did→

It chose (b). It opened an independent investigation led by a committee of independent directors with outside counsel, and said it still had confidence in Huang. Reporting later said the general counsel had to press directors to open it. On 10 November the board hired former acting US Attorney Joon Kim and Wachtell Lipton to lead it.

⏸ Decision point 2 · 11 November

New reports say Huang also had a relationship with an Nvidia executive who reported directly to him. Directors reportedly believe he was not candid with them.

Does this change the calculation? Is a relationship with a direct report a removal offence regardless of performance?

Decide first, then see what the board didWhat the board did→

It accepted Huang’s resignation as president, CEO and director. On 12 November it announced that Kress would be interim CEO while staying CFO, and that Jones would be the company’s first-ever board chair. The emergency plan worked: Kress took over the same day.

⏸ Decision point 3 · 17 November

Huang publicly offers to return. The petition passes 180,000 signatures, including about 8,800 people who say they are current employees. Elon Musk backs it. On the same day, Kress reports a record quarter. Investors on the earnings call want to know if Huang is coming back.

Should the chair commit publicly either way, or keep the board’s options open?

Decide first, then see what the board didWhat the board did→

Jones told investors the board “does not intend to reinstate” Huang, and Nvidia hired Heidrick & Struggles to find a permanent CEO.

⏸ Decision point 4 · 22–23 November

President Trump says publicly that he discussed Huang with Xi Jinping and wants him given a second chance, then posts that he hopes the board rehires him. The investigation is still open.

Your options

  • ahold the 17 November position
  • bwait for the investigation, then decide
  • creinstate him with formal conditions or on leave until the findings
  • dreinstate him now

What would you do? What does each option cost, and who bears the cost?

Decide first, then see what the board didWhat the board did→

It chose (d). On 23 November the board unanimously reinstated Huang as president, CEO and director. It said discussions had satisfied it on his “candor and ability to lead”, and that senior US government officials had told it his return posed no national security risk. It also created an independent-director National Security and Export Control Committee and began a search for a Chief Compliance Officer. Jones stayed chair, and Kress went back to being CFO. Trump claimed credit, and Semafor reported he had joined the board meeting by phone. The investigation had not finished.

Part 2

My argument

The succession plan worked. The board didn’t.

Most commentary says Nvidia had no succession plan. I made the same point in my first reaction, and I was wrong. When Huang left, Kress took over the same day and delivered a record quarter (Exhibit 3). On the question every board should be able to answer, who runs this company if the CEO is gone tomorrow?, Nvidia had an answer that worked.

The failure was at a second, harder layer: would the board act on its own judgment once the envelope was open? It didn’t. Within a week, under pressure from a petition, a falling stock and the President, it reversed a commitment it had made to investors, before its own investigation reported.

That’s the indispensability trap. The danger isn’t a strong CEO, it’s an irreplaceable one, and only the board can stop a CEO from becoming irreplaceable. Nvidia’s company could survive without Huang. Its board could not bring itself to act as though that were true.

How a strong CEO becomes an irreplaceable one

Strength isn’t the problem. Nvidia exists because Huang made bold bets for three decades, and most of them were right. The problem is what forms around a leader like that when no one deliberately pushes back. Exhibit 2 shows a board with no leader of its own until the crisis. Exhibit 4 shows a market that priced the person, not the institution: one five-sentence statement moved about $100 billion. Exhibit 5 shows that other boards have removed CEOs for a relationship with an employee, and every Nvidia manager has now seen a different standard at the top.

None of this is dramatic on its own. Together it produces a company that is commercially very strong and institutionally fragile.

Results vs. resilience

Results are what a company produces with its leader in place. Resilience is what’s left if the leader is gone tomorrow. Nvidia was tested on resilience in November. Its operations passed. Its governance did not. Management delivers the first; boards are paid to look after the second.

Steve Jobs: both sides of the trap

Jobs is usually held up as the ultimate indispensable founder, but he’s really the best example of escaping the trap. The first time, Apple had nothing to fall back on without him. The second time, he let Cook run Apple under pressure, and Cook later did the same for Ternus. The company that once couldn’t survive without its founder now hands over power routinely.

The open question is which Jobs Jensen Huang will be now that he’s back.

The fair case for the board

The board’s defenders have real points. Huang has not been charged. He has admitted serious mistakes and agreed to cooperate and to testify. Shareholders and many employees wanted him back. The board added real structures (Exhibit 2), and in a fast AI race continuity has value.

My view is that these are reasons for patience and due process, not for skipping them. A board that is confident in what the investigation will find can wait for it. A board that isn’t confident has every reason to wait.

Part 3

Lessons and questions

Lessons

  1. 01
    Succession has two layers. Have a name for tomorrow, and be ready to act on it. Most plans fail at the second layer.
  2. 02
    Separate the chair before the crisis, not during it.
  3. 03
    Finish the process you start. An investigation you cut short protects no one, including the CEO.
  4. 04
    A board’s word is an asset. Commitments that reverse in days cost credibility with investors, regulators, auditors and employees.
  5. 05
    Private conduct stops being private when it involves a direct report, a major customer or national security.
  6. 06
    Performance is not a governance defense. Strong results justify patience, not exemptions.
  7. 07
    For founders: make your own period of one-person rule as short as possible. Build leaders who can do your job, and let them prove it before they need to.

There’s a saying in India: if you cannot be true to your own, how can you be true to anyone else? Whatever one thinks of how it applies to a CEO’s private life, it applies to institutions without question. A board that cannot hold to its own decision cannot be relied on by anyone else.

For your leadership team

Questions for your leadership team

  • ?If our CEO left tomorrow, who would run the company, and have they ever done it under pressure?
  • ?Who could lead us for the next decade? Is that the same person?
  • ?Does our board have a leader who isn’t the CEO?
  • ?Has our board ever made a decision our CEO strongly opposed? What happened?
  • ?If our stock moved 3% on one person’s statement, what would that tell us?

For classes and workshops

Discussion questions

  1. Go back to Decision point 4. Which option would you have chosen, and what would you have said publicly?
  2. Was the 17 November “will not reinstate” statement a mistake in itself?
  3. How should a board weigh a petition from employees and shareholders against its own process?
  4. What weight, if any, should a board give to a head of government’s views on its CEO?
  5. Kress kept the company running. Is she now a stronger or weaker candidate for the next succession?
  6. Which of the changes in Exhibit 2 would actually constrain the CEO, and how would you test that in a year?
