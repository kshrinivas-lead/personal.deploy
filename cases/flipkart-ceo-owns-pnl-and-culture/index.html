A CEO Owns P&L and Culture

What Kalyan Krishnamurthy’s exit from Flipkart teaches about accountability for causes that seem external

By Shrinivas Kulkarni·As of 28 November 2026Developing — investigations open

Real constraints are not a defence. A CEO owns the outcome, including the parts that seem to be caused from outside.

How to read this. Part 1 tells the story neutrally, with pause points (⏸) where you can decide what you would have done. The exhibits hold the evidence. Part 2 is my argument. Part 3 is for your own leadership team.

Some figures below come from unnamed sources and are labelled as reported. The kickback allegations mentioned are unproven, and no report alleges that Krishnamurthy took any payment.

Part 1

The case

On 25 November 2026 Flipkart announced that Kalyan Krishnamurthy, its CEO since 2017, would leave on 4 December (Reuters). He had led Flipkart through Walmart’s $16 bn deal in 2018 and kept it ahead of Amazon, with an estimated 50–60% of Indian e-commerce (ClickPost, citing ICICI Securities). Flipkart gave no reason.

A new boss

In February 2026 a former Walmart CFO, Chris Nicholas, took over Walmart International (People Matters). Flipkart had reportedly ended 2025 close to EBITDA breakeven.

⏸ Pause 1 · February 2026

Your new boss is a numbers person, and you are close to breakeven.

Do you keep running your plan, or re-agree targets and own them publicly?

Culture warnings

Walmart’s first fraud probe at Flipkart, around 2024, ended with at least three employees fired (Storyboard18). A second followed in 2026, and a third in October, over alleged kickbacks of more than ₹150 crore (NewsBytes).

⏸ Pause 2 · After the first probe

After the first case, do you fire the people, or change the system that let it happen?

P&L pressure

In May 2026 Walmart reportedly deferred the IPO and asked Flipkart to reach breakeven by March 2027 (Zerodha). Flipkart then launched food delivery and microdramas (Business Today). FY26 filings showed a group loss of ₹8,468 crore (Inc42).

⏸ Pause 3 · May 2026

Breakeven is now the condition for an IPO.

Do you prune loss-makers, or keep investing to fight quick-commerce rivals?

People

Former senior executives, and then about 1,000 former employees, wrote to Walmart’s board seeking liquidity for stock options they had held for years (Outlook Business). At least 14 VPs and SVPs left in a year (Inc42).

⏸ Pause 4 · 2026

The IPO delay and the liquidity backlog are real, and your owner decided the IPO timing.

Do you treat them as the owner’s problem, or present your own plan regardless?

The decision

Sources told Business Standard that Walmart worried about growth, losses and the unresolved stock options. They also said he was held responsible, as CEO, for failing to prevent the kickbacks. The handover took about a week. In public, Walmart thanked him for his leadership and governance.

⏸ Pause 5 · November 2026

You are the owner.

Do you change the CEO now, set conditions, or agree a planned transition?

Part 2

My argument

A CEO owns two things: the P&L and the culture. Everything else is a means to those two.

Flipkart’s record in 2026 tests both. On P&L, the company was reportedly close to breakeven at the end of 2025, then drifted well off its targets while adding new businesses. On culture, it had three fraud probes in under three years, senior exits, and former staff writing to the owner’s board.

Many of the causes looked external: fierce competition, an IPO delay decided by Walmart, a liquidity backlog outside the CEO’s hands. But follow each one back (Exhibit 2). The IPO was reportedly delayed because of the losses. Liquidity reportedly depended on the financials. The kickbacks happened inside the company’s own processes. Every road leads back to P&L or culture, which is exactly the CEO’s job. Wells Fargo shows what happens when a leader treats a culture problem as someone else’s (Exhibit 3).

The case for the other side

It is a strong one. The constraints were real and partly the owner’s. The goalposts moved when a new boss arrived in 2026, and GE’s Flannery was famously compared to “a coach fired after one season.” Flipkart’s core stayed strong, and nobody accuses Krishnamurthy of wrongdoing.

My answer

The goalposts did move, and that is why the first 90 days with a new boss matter: you re-agree the targets and own them. Ten years is not one season. A strong core makes persistent losses a choice rather than fate. And the argument stands on confirmed facts: the loss, the repeated probes and the letters to the owner’s board.

Part 3

Lessons and questions

Lessons

  1. 01
    A CEO owns two things: P&L and culture.
  2. 02
    Trace every “external” cause back to what you control.
  3. 03
    Real constraints still need your own plan.
  4. 04
    When your boss changes, re-agree the targets and own them.
  5. 05
    Culture shows up as repeats: repeat fraud, repeat exits, people going over your head.
  6. 06
    Results buy patience, not immunity.

For your leadership team

Questions for your own leadership team

  • ?Which of our shortfalls do we explain mainly by outside forces? What part of each do we control?
  • ?If our board or owner changed tomorrow, which targets would we need to re-agree?
  • ?What would our people write if they wrote to our board?
  • ?Which failure has happened here more than once?

For classes and workshops

Using the pauses

Take each pause point. Decide, then compare your choice with what happened, and with the Wells Fargo and GE cases (Exhibit 3).
